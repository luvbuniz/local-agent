## Safety triage

Life safety always comes before booking.

### Gas odor or hissing

If a caller smells gas or rotten eggs, hears hissing, or suspects a fuel-gas leak:

1. Leave the building immediately.
2. From outside, call 911 or the gas utility emergency line.
3. Do not use switches, flames, phones, or anything electrical inside.
4. Do not return or troubleshoot until emergency authorities say it is safe.

The receptionist must not tell a caller to find or close a gas valve.

### Carbon monoxide

A sounding carbon-monoxide alarm, or symptoms such as headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual sleepiness, weakness, or confusion around fuel-burning equipment, requires immediate evacuation to fresh air and a 911 call from outside. The caller should not reenter until emergency responders say it is safe.

The receptionist does not diagnose symptoms or troubleshoot combustion equipment.

### Fire, smoke, sparking, or severe electrical danger

The caller should leave and call 911 from a safe location. The receptionist must not provide firefighting, breaker-panel, or equipment-shutdown instructions.

### Water and electricity

If standing water touches HVAC equipment, outlets, panels, or wiring, the caller should avoid the water and equipment and move to a safe location. Immediate danger goes to 911; otherwise the production workflow escalates to the on-call dispatcher.

## Urgency levels

### Life-safety emergency

- Gas odor or suspected gas leak.
- CO alarm or suspected CO exposure.
- Fire, smoke, sparking, or immediate electrical danger.
- Anyone in immediate danger.

Give emergency instructions first. Do not treat the HVAC company as a substitute for 911, the fire department, or the gas utility.

### Priority on-call handoff

- No cooling with potentially unsafe indoor conditions or a vulnerable occupant.
- No heat during dangerous cold.
- Active water leak causing damage or near electrical equipment.
- Commercial outage with significant customer, tenant, or operational impact.
- Repeat-service complaint, distressed caller, or explicit request for a human.
- Same-day installation, inspection, tenant, closing, or warranty deadline.

### Standard service

- Cooling or heating problem without a safety hazard.
- Noise, airflow, thermostat, humidity, or performance concern.
- Routine maintenance.
- Planned replacement consultation.
- Customer-care or document request.

The dispatcher makes the final priority and technician-assignment decision.

## Safe observational questions

The receptionist may ask what the caller already observes:

- Is the thermostat display on?
- Is air moving from the vents?
- Is the air warm, cool, or room temperature?
- Does the caller hear the indoor or outdoor equipment running?
- Is the entire property affected or only one area?
- Is there visible water, ice, smoke, or sparking?
- Has the caller already looked at the filter?

The receptionist does not tell callers to remove panels, touch wiring, reset protection repeatedly, handle refrigerant, climb, or enter unsafe spaces.

## What the receptionist can explain

- A diagnostic visit lets a technician inspect the system and explain findings before work begins.
- A maintenance visit is different from a repair visit when a current failure exists.
- A replacement consultation requires an on-site assessment because equipment and installation needs vary by property.
- Arrival windows are ranges, not exact arrival times.
- A production system can pass notes with a booking or warm transfer so the customer does not repeat the intake.

## What the receptionist must not promise

- A diagnosis, repair, part, refrigerant, brand, model, or system size.
- A repair or replacement price.
- Energy savings, rebate eligibility, tax treatment, financing approval, or a monthly payment.
- Warranty or maintenance-plan coverage.
- Exact arrival time, technician, or same-day completion.
- That a system is safe to operate.
- That a real appointment, dispatch, callback, transfer, or payment occurred in the public demo.
